Chapter one

Owner or renter?

Have you noticed your AI is starting to know you?

You ask ChatGPT for an email, and it already knows how you like them written. It remembers your business, your clients, the thing you mentioned last month. And in the space of about ten weeks, four of the biggest AI companies each launched an agent: xAI's Grok Bot, Meta's Muse, OpenAI's Dots and Anthropic's Claude Duet. An agent is an AI app that does the work instead of just answering. Which one are you supposed to pick?

If you already use one, keep using it. They are good, and there is nothing to set up, because they run on the AI company's computer, not yours. Remember that last part.

Because while you use them, they are quietly building a map of you. And the most valuable thing your AI builds is not your chats. It is the map: your clients, what they like, what you promised, which decisions worked, and how it all connects.

Say you type "Reply to Sarah." With the map, your AI knows she is your biggest client, hates long emails, and is owed a call on Friday. Without it, you explain everything again.

So who keeps the map? Try exporting it. You keep the chat history and a list of memory notes. They keep the links between those notes, the months of context, and everything the AI learned about how you work.

If you closed the app tomorrow, what would you still have?

The way I see it, the future splits into two camps. Owners and renters.

Renters keep their map on someone else's computer. They are stuck with whatever AI their app chooses: Dots only works with OpenAI's own AI, and Grok Bot does not let you choose. Every time they switch apps, they start over. And they will keep being told the same thing: you will own nothing and be happy.

Owners keep the map on their own computer. They plug in any AI they want, and when they switch, they keep everything.

That is me. I plug in whichever AI is best that month: ChatGPT, Claude, Grok. When a better one ships, I swap it, and my map stays put. (Let them fight. I just use the best one.) My map is the one thing I built that made every new AI launch boring to me. In a good way.

I still rent the AI itself. The best AI models today, the engines that do the thinking, run on the big companies' computers, and yes, they see my data. There is no other choice yet. But my map is built on a computer I own. I rent the model. I own the map.

That is what this guide is about: building your own AI second brain, a home for your map on a computer you own, with any AI plugged in. Keep the apps you like. Just make sure your map lives at home, not only inside them. Owner or renter? You can still choose.

So if the agent apps are not the valuable part, what are they? And the bigger question, the one everyone is quietly afraid of: will AI end up replacing you anyway? Let me answer both with an animal.

Chapter two

Extended, not replaced

An octopus has nine brains.

One central brain and a mini-brain in every arm. The central brain could be sleeping while the arms are still functioning, gathering food, preventing threats. The arms are not a threat to the octopus. They do not replace it. Each arm senses, reacts, and handles its own work, while the center decides where the whole creature goes. One animal is running eight things at once, and the center is more powerful because of the arms, not less.

That is the honest answer to the question everyone is afraid of. AI is not what replaces you. AI is your arms.

Look at how the split actually works. You keep what only you can do: your intent, your values, your taste, the final call. AI extends everything else: the memory, the analysis, the drafting, the execution. You do not get replaced. You get extended. Grok Bot, Muse, Dots and Claude Duet are all arms, and good ones. Anyone can rent the arms. You want to own the second brain they all plug into.

There is a word for a person extended by machines this way: bionic.

But here is what nobody tells you, and it is the entire reason this guide exists. The arms only work if they are connected to the centre. Eight brilliant arms with no centre is not an octopus. It is chaos. Remember that line, because it is exactly where I went wrong first, and it is exactly where almost everyone using AI today is stuck right now.

Chapter nine

One shared memory

The fix was embarrassingly simple. I remembered a book I read many years ago: Building a Second Brain, by Tiago Forte.

As someone with ADHD, I tend to be very forgetful. I would write down lots of notes, bookmark and save stuff on social media, only to never open it again. I was exploring a system that would help me remember everything.

The book taught me a system called CODE. Capture, Organize, Distill, Express.

  • Capture everything important into an inbox folder
  • Organize it into Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives (aka PARA)
  • Distill it into atomic notes (like taking a meeting transcript and distilling ten reusable points)
  • Then use those points to Express new output (new scripts, new ideas, new plans)

The system was simple. The execution was hard. I had to manually capture my own notes, then sit down once a week to organize and distill them before I could use them. Nahhh. My ADHD brain said doomscrolling was more fun.

Then an idea hit me: what if I let my AI agents manage and use this second brain as our shared memory?

One place where everything I know and my company knows lives. Every decision and why I made it. Every call, transcribed. Every client's history and quirks. Every number. Everything that worked and everything that flopped. Every agent reads from it before acting, and writes back into it after. The day it existed, the arms finally connected to a brain. The mess became an octopus. That is the day I became bionic.

Now, the part that surprises everyone I show this to. That shared memory, the company brain, is not technology.

It is just a folder.

Chapter eleven

The loop that learns

A folder full of files is still just a filing cabinet. What turned mine into a living brain is a loop that runs through it. This is the climax of the whole guide, so slow down for two minutes.

Not better tools. Better architecture. The loop has five beats. I call it CLOSE: Capture, Link, Organise, Signal, Execute. The last beat feeds the first, so the loop closes on itself.

Capture. Your business already produces everything the brain needs. It just evaporates. So everything flows in, from every place information lives: your inbox, your WhatsApp and Slack threads, meeting recordings, your notes, the numbers in your ad account. Calls get transcribed the day they happen. Nothing valuable dies just because you were busy.

You set up a capture agent just to do this every 30 minutes or every hour (depending on how big your company is). This is also known as a "cron job": agents that wake up every X minutes, hours, days or weeks to do something.

Link. This is the beat every notes system misses, and the reason my old one died. A WhatsApp thread, a call transcript, a proposal and a closed deal are four files in four places. To a filing cabinet they are four unrelated things. To a brain they are one customer. Link ties every new piece to the client, deal, project or person it belongs to, so when you ask about a client you get the whole story: what they said on the call, what you quoted, what they replied on WhatsApp, and whether they paid.

Mine is a linking pass that runs the moment a file lands: it gets tied to who and what it is about before it is filed.

Organise. The linked pieces get filed onto the nine shelves, each with a date and a source. Why bother? Because a fact you cannot find at the moment you need it may as well not exist. Filing is what turns a pile into an answer.

Mine is a daily triage agent that files everything in the inbox folder into one of the nine folders.

Signal. Distill first, then detect. Every long thing gets boiled down to the few lines that matter. A sixty-minute client call becomes five lines: what they promised, what worried them, what changed, the number, the next step. Nobody, human or AI, acts on a pile. They act on the five lines. Then the brain reads across many of those five-line pieces until something appears that no single piece contained. Twelve sales calls, and the same objection sitting in nine of them. No one call told me that. The pile did, once it was sitting in one place. That one sentence rewrote my pitch, and it is the kind of thing your competitors never see about their own business.

Or across departments. Marketing reported cost per lead went down. Sales reported conversion went up. Customer support reported complaints went up. Maybe there is a link between those three numbers? Maybe the ad creative overpromised and produced higher complaints and refunds? You would not know without this system, and big companies pay billions of dollars to figure it out. Now it is available to you for the price of a ChatGPT subscription.

A signal is not a weekly summary, and it is not the AI having a feeling. It is a number that moved, with enough cases behind it to trust it: nine calls out of twelve, not one. My daily sweep does the boiling down at the end of every day, and my signal agent reads across it weekly and reports what it finds back into the brain.

Execute. The agents pull from the brain and act: proposals, posts, follow-ups, ads, slide decks, the stalled deal that gets a dated follow-up task. This is where everything pays off. Ask a bare AI for an output and you get the same generic AI slop your competitor produces, because it draws on nothing but the public internet.

Ask an agent that reads your brain, and the output quotes what the client said on Tuesday's call, prices from your rate card, and shows proof from your archive. Generic in, generic out. Your brain in, only-you out. And every action gets logged: what changed, and what happened next.

Chapter fourteen

Hire them like employees

You create an AI agent the way you would hire a person. Because that is what it is: a new member of staff who happens to read the entire company brain on day one.

Start with an org chart, exactly like a real company. Draw the departments your business already has: marketing, sales, delivery, finance, operations. The same departments as the Areas shelf in your brain, and that is not a coincidence. Then hire one agent per department, one at a time, each owning one job and one number, like real staff. The marketing agent owns cost per lead. The delivery agent owns retention. The finance agent owns cash.

A human hire spends three months learning how you do things. Your AI hire reads the brain and starts the same day, already knowing every decision you have made, every client's history, every standard you hold. And every piece of work it produces goes back into the brain with the result attached, which means next month's version of that agent is smarter than this month's. So is every other agent on the chart, because they all read the same brain.

When a human employee leaves, their experience walks out the door. Here, the experience stays in the building forever. I will be honest about how much peace that gives me: I am no longer worried when someone on my team leaves, because the knowledge never leaves with them anymore.

One rule keeps the roster clean as it grows: agents own an outcome, skills are just abilities anyone can pick up. A "writing agent" owns nothing and needs briefing from scratch every time. An agent that owns booked calls is a colleague. Here is the shape a full roster grows into. You will not start with all seven. Neither did I.

Chapter fifteen

What mine looks like, honestly

Theory is cheap, so here is my actual machine, described plainly, including the part most people selling AI will not say.

  • It lives on a small computer at home that never sleeps. Not my laptop. My laptop closes, travels and sleeps, so anything living on it is not staff.
  • The chief of staff reads its compass before it does anything. What we are chasing this quarter, which number is behind, what is already in flight.
  • Every morning it picks the move that is furthest behind and ships that one thing. Not a dashboard. Not twelve suggestions. One finished thing, and a note saying why.
  • Six agents sit under it, one number each: audience, leads, revenue, retention, cash, and whether things are actually running.
  • The brain is plain files on a machine I own. If every AI company I use went under tomorrow, I would still have my company's brain in the morning. New engine, same car.
This is what survives all the AI hype.
The steering wheel, in one sentence
Never report a problem alone. Explain it in human terms, give two or three real options with their trade-offs, recommend one, and take the next step inside the boundary you have been given.

Without that line you get: "I could not send the invoice." With it you get: "The invoice bounced, their billing email changed. I can resend to the contact on the last paid invoice, ask in the shared thread, or hold until Monday. I would resend, and I have drafted it. Want it?"

Same worker. Same AI. One sentence of written-down judgement. I wrote it once, several engines ago, and every worker since has inherited it.

And the honest version, because this matters: it drafts, queues and chases. I tap send. AI does about 90% of the work and I own the last 10%. Anyone telling you their agents run a business unattended is selling you a fake dream.
